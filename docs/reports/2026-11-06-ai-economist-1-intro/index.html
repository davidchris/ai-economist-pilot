The idea: learn a tax policy by simulation

Most economists meet optimal taxation through the Mirrlees problem. A government wants to redistribute, but it can only observe income, and it cannot see the ability or effort behind that income. Taxing income discourages work, so equality comes at a cost in output. Theory gives elegant answers, such as the Saez formula, by assuming a tractable model of how people respond.

In 2020 a team of researchers, mostly at Salesforce, tried a different route in The AI Economist. They built a small simulated economy, filled it with AI workers who learn to earn a living, and added an AI tax planner that learns tax rates by trial and error. Nobody writes down a labor supply elasticity. If the planner raises a rate and workers respond by working less or shifting income across periods, the planner discovers that from the results.

The paper reported a 16% better equality-productivity trade-off than the next-best baseline, the Saez framework, and 47% more equality than a free market at an 11% cost in productivity. A later study appeared in Science Advances.

This repository is an independent attempt to recreate that 2020 simulation study, using Salesforce's original economic simulator with a newly written PyTorch trainer. The simulator is unchanged except for one NumPy compatibility fix. The original training stack (Python 3.7, TensorFlow 1.14, Ray 0.8.4) is hard to run today, and the upstream project was archived in 2023.

The result, up front

The project has not reproduced the paper. The pipeline works and workers learn to produce at full labor cost, but the learned tax planner has not learned anything useful yet. This series explains the method, what the project got right, and how each failure was diagnosed. It says nothing against the paper's result, because the latest run used about 1% of the paper's tax-training budget.

The world in one picture

The economy is a 25×25 grid with four workers. Wood and stone appear on fixed source tiles. A worker can walk, collect resources, trade them on a market for coin, and combine one wood and one stone into a house, which pays coin. Some workers earn far more per house than others. Workers start in different quadrants, and the best builder starts in the only quadrant with no resources at all, which gives trade a reason to exist.

One episode lasts 1,000 steps. Every 100 steps, income earned during that period is taxed and the revenue is shared equally among the four workers. Part 2 covers every rule.

Six concepts you need

Agent

A decision maker. There are four workers and one planner. Think of them as the households and the government in a model, except that their behavior is learned and not derived from first-order conditions.

Environment

The rules of the world, here a simulator called Foundation. It takes everyone's actions and returns the next state plus a reward for each agent.

Reward

A number that scores the last step. Workers receive the change in their utility, and the planner receives the change in social welfare. These are the objective functions.

Policy

A function from what an agent sees to probabilities over actions. It is the agent's decision rule, and also the thing being estimated.

Neural network

The flexible function used to represent a policy. In this project it is about 387,000 parameters for workers and 1.7 million for the planner. Part 3 builds it up from logistic regression.

Reinforcement learning

A way to fit a policy with no labels: try actions, see the rewards, and raise the probability of actions that went better than expected. Part 4 explains the algorithm, called PPO.

Translating from what you already know

If you have fitted models for a living, most of the vocabulary maps onto something familiar. The important difference is in the last column of the first row: the model produces its own training data.

One consequence surprises people from a tabular background. There is no external dataset. The economy generates all of its own experience, and a full run in this project creates tens of millions of records that nobody ever looks at individually. The expensive part is running the simulator, and the GPU sits mostly idle.

Why training happens in two phases

Starting everything at once does not work. A worker with random behavior earns almost nothing, so a planner taxing that worker gets no useful signal. A planner with random rates would also confuse a worker that has not yet learned what a house is. The paper uses a curriculum, a schedule that makes the problem gradually harder:

  1. Phase one, workers only, no tax. Labor starts almost free and its cost ramps up to the full level, so workers discover that building pays before they discover that working hurts.
  2. Phase two, workers and planner together. Labor is at full cost. The planner starts learning with rates capped at 10%, and the cap rises to 100% over time, so workers can adapt gradually.

Both ramps turned out to be central to this project's story. The first was the cause of an early collapse. The second is where the current failure shows up.

What this project set out to do

The goal is narrow: recreate the 2020 simulation study while keeping Foundation's economic mechanics exactly as published, so any difference has to come from training and not from a changed economy. Reproducing a deep RL result is a large job on a shared machine, so the work has gone in stages. Each run is limited to two CPU cores, 8 GiB of RAM and ten minutes, and long runs are chained from exact checkpoints.

Progress so far

The project has moved from "does it run?" through "is it trustworthy?" to "does it learn?". The first answer was yes, the second took most of the engineering, and the third is only partly yes.

Where it stands today

The latest experiment took three independently pretrained worker populations and continued each for 4.4 million steps under three treatments: no tax, the scaled 2018 US federal schedule, and a learning planner. Each policy was then frozen and scored on ten episodes it had never seen, at full labor cost and with no cap on tax rates. Output is total coin produced. Welfare is the paper's objective: output per worker multiplied by equality. Worker return is the utility a worker gains over an episode.

The fixed-tax result is also not settled: its welfare gap to the free market moved from −19.4 to −65.9 between the two evaluation points, so treat that ranking as provisional. Three seeds make the confidence intervals descriptive at best.

Established

The pipeline trains, resumes exactly and evaluates on held-out episodes. Workers sustain production at full labor cost on three seeds. The earlier collapse came from the labor ramp.

Not established

Any reproduction of the paper. Convergence of any policy. Why the planner stays close to random. Any comparison with the Saez baseline, which has not been implemented.

The next step is a cheap diagnostic: replace the planner with uniformly random rates and see whether the same collapse appears. That separates "random taxes destroy the economy" from "the planner has some additional problem". Only then does it make sense to tune the planner or spend on a larger run.
