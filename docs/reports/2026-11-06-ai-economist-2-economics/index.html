The textbook problem

Mirrlees (1971) framed income taxation as a problem of hidden information. People differ in ability, ability is unobservable, and the government can only tax income. A tax on ability would not distort anything, but an income tax lowers the return to effort, so redistribution costs output.

Saez (2001) restated the solution in terms economists can estimate. At income z, the optimal marginal rate is higher when society puts little welfare weight on people earning above z, and lower when income responds strongly to taxes:

τ(z) = (1 − Ḡ(z)) / (1 − Ḡ(z) + a(z)·e(z))

Here Ḡ(z) is the average welfare weight on people above z, a(z) describes the income distribution above z (a smaller value means a thicker upper tail, which supports a higher rate), and e(z) is the elasticity. At the very top, the welfare weight goes to zero and the rate becomes 1/(1 + a·e).

The AI Economist paper's pitch is that real behavior strains these assumptions: workers can time income across tax periods, specialize, and trade with each other. A model-free learner needs no estimated elasticity and no static assumption. It only needs a simulator that contains those behaviors, and the price is that its answer is only as good as the simulator and the training.

In the paper's experiments, the Saez baseline estimates elasticity by regression on recent income and tax observations, and it was the strongest baseline. The Foundation code includes that baseline. This project has not implemented or validated it yet, so no comparison with Saez appears below.

Who lives in this economy

Four workers live on the map below. Each has a build skill that sets coin per house and is fixed by rank across episodes: 11.33, 13.27, 16.47 and 22.25. Those numbers come from averaging ordered draws of a clipped Pareto(4) distribution, scaled so pay runs from 10 to 30 coin per house. Skills are the source of inequality, and a worker's skill determines where they start, as set out in the table.

Each worker also gets a gathering skill, redrawn each episode: a chance of receiving a bonus resource from a tile with no extra labor. Both skills are private. A worker observes its own, and the planner never does. That is the Mirrlees information problem, built directly into the observation design.

Production: how coin gets made

At each step a worker chooses exactly one of 50 actions: do nothing (1), build (1), place a market order (44), or move one tile (4). Invalid actions, such as building without materials, are masked out.

That last point gives a useful identity: total output is exactly the sum of house payments, so output per house reveals who is doing the building. Empty source tiles respawn with 1% probability per step, so resources are renewable but scarce near any one location. There is one mechanical oddity worth knowing. Collection runs even on a do-nothing step, so if a resource respawns under a stationary worker, that worker gathers it and pays labor. That is why a pure no-op policy earns about −1 and not zero.

The market for wood and stone

Wood and stone trade on a continuous double auction, like a small commodity exchange. A worker posts a bid or ask at an integer price from 0 to 10 coin. Coin or resources committed to an open order go into escrow. Orders expire after 50 steps, and a worker can hold at most 5 open orders per resource. When a bid and an ask cross, they trade at the price of whichever order arrived first.

The 44 market actions are 2 resources × 2 sides × 11 prices. Each order costs a small amount of labor, set so its utility cost is 0.05 in the paper's configuration. Workers see the order book and price history. Nobody tells them equilibrium prices or that trade is useful. They have to discover it.

Preferences: utility and the cost of labor

Each worker maximises the same preference form, with diminishing marginal utility in coin and a constant marginal cost of labor:

ui = (ci1−η − 1) / (1 − η) − w · 0.21 · Li,   η = 0.23

Here c is coin held after tax, L is cumulative labor, and w is a training weight that ramps from 0 to 1 during pretraining and equals exactly 1 in every evaluation. This is a CRRA form in coin, also called isoelastic utility. Only coin held at the end of the episode ultimately matters, because utility is a stock and there is no consumption flow, no savings return and no price level.

The learner receives the change in utility at each step as its reward, so rewards sum to total utility gain over the episode. The discount factor of 0.998 per step adds mild impatience.

The tax system

Tax is assessed on income per tax period, meaning the change in a worker's coin over 100 steps. The seven brackets use 2018 US federal cutoffs scaled down by 1,000. Revenue is returned as an equal lump sum to all four workers, so the system redistributes even with a flat rate.

Worked example

Under the fixed US rates of 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%, a worker earning 100 coin in a period owes 9.7×10% + 29.775×12% + 44.725×22% + 15.8×24%, which comes to 18.17 coin. If the other three workers earned nothing, each worker gets 18.17 ÷ 4 = 4.54 back. The high earner's net rate is about 13.6%, and the others gain 4.54 each.

Three tax regimes

  • Free market. Taxes disabled.
  • Fixed US tax. The rates above, in every period. This baseline tests the machinery with a familiar schedule. It is not meant to be optimal for this economy.
  • Learned tax. A planner chooses for each bracket at the start of each period from 22 options: keep the current rate, or set any of 21 rates from 0% to 100% in steps of 5%. That is 227 joint choices, ten times per episode.

The planner sees the full map, all public fields, market state and last-period incomes. It does not see skills or labor. During training, a ceiling limits the available rates: in the current setup it starts at 10% and reaches 100% after 900 completed episodes per economy, or 3.6M steps across four economies. Both tax treatments train under that ceiling. Evaluation removes it.

The planner's objective

The paper and this project use a simple product of equality and output per worker:

welfare = (1 − Gininorm(c)) × (Σi ci / 4)

The Gini coefficient is normalized so that one worker holding everything gives exactly 0 equality, even with only four people. Here is how the arithmetic behaves:

Two features deserve attention. First, this objective contains no utility and no labor. A planner that works everyone to exhaustion is not penalised for it, which differs from a standard utilitarian welfare function. Foundation offers alternatives, such as inverse-income weights, but they are not used in these experiments. Second, equality is 1 when nobody has anything. Always read equality next to output. In this project's collapsed economies, equality reads as a perfect 1.0 with welfare at 0.

This is specialization that no one programmed. The top builder starts with nothing nearby, buys materials from the others and builds about 69 of 100 houses. It is far from an efficient economy, though. In these policies 57–59% of actions are still no-ops and about a quarter are market orders. The project has not shown convergence, and it has not compared this productivity with the paper's.

Fixed-tax rates top out at 37%. Learned rates often exceed that in the lowest brackets, where every worker pays them. A working hypothesis, not yet tested, is that workers facing high, unpredictable rates find doing nothing to be their best response, after which the planner gets no useful signal because nothing is being produced. An experiment with uniformly random rates and no planner at all would test the first half directly.

What the model leaves out

Even a perfect reproduction would answer a narrower question than real tax policy:

  • Four workers with fixed skill ranks on one fixed map. No firms, capital, savings return, migration, demographics or life-cycle.
  • One output good. A house is just a coin payment, and there is no demand side.
  • A narrow avoidance channel. Timing income across periods is possible. Evasion, or reclassifying income, is not.
  • Trained behavior. Workers are RL policies optimised over very many simulated lifetimes, so they are not models of bounded human decision making. The paper did run a follow-up with human participants, but this project targets only its simulation study.
  • A planner with an equal-rebate rule. Only rates are chosen. Revenue always returns equally, and no public goods exist.

What the framework offers in exchange is a controlled environment for testing whether a learned policy can handle strategic response, with every allowed response written down in code.
